# Diagram brief — <moduleId>

Six fields. Most take ten minutes. Where a field can be drafted from the module's
`content.ts` and `questions.ts`, I'll draft it and you correct it — correcting is far
cheaper than authoring.

## Teaching claim

> One sentence: what can the learner say after looking at this that they could not
> before?

If this can't be written, the diagram has no job and the frame should be a chart, a
readout, or nothing. This is the field that makes a diagram *educational* rather than
decorative.

## Register

`anatomical` | `schematic` | `graph`

Per the existing rule: a module whose subject is an ORGAN draws the organ; a module
whose subject is a GRAPH stays a graph; a module whose subject is CELLULAR or
MOLECULAR stays schematic, because drawing it as gross anatomy lies about the scale.

## Must-show structures

The named parts that have to be visible for the teaching claim to land.

## Control → visible correlate

| Control | What moves in the picture |
|---|---|
|  |  |

Every slider gets a row. A control with no structure to show belongs in a group
labelled as a model parameter rather than being faked. This enforces at design time
the rule the audit currently catches only afterwards.

## Source of truth

The figure, text, or dataset the anatomy and the numbers are checked against.

This is the **accuracy** leg, and nothing in the repo currently records it — which
means no diagram's claims can be re-checked without redoing the reasoning. A citation
here is also what makes the content defensible commercially.

## Must not reveal

Patterns the practice questions ask about. A diagram that names the pattern answers
the question being asked a few hundred pixels below it.
